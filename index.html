What the work actually is

The job description says administration. The accountability is assurance.

In a regulated organisation, the risk isn't that training doesn't happen — it's that you can't prove it happened, to the right people, at the right time, against the current document. Everything below is built to close that gap.

“Allocate correct mandatory and role-specific training; monitor refresher periods”
→
Own a controlled training matrix and a live expiry engine An expired refresher isn't an admin slip — it's a regulatory exposure sitting on a named person.
“Support audits with accurate records and supporting documentation”
→
Keep an evidence pack that is always current, never assembled in a panic Audit readiness is a continuous state. Scrambling is itself a signal of weak control.
“Set up LMS events, upload materials and assessments, maintain version control”
→
Run the learning system as a controlled document environment Training delivered against a superseded document is a finding in its own right.
“Chase trainers for missing or incorrect attendance registers”
→
Close the gap between training delivered and training evidenced Undocumented training is, to an auditor, training that never happened.
“Take minutes, maintain action trackers, follow up outstanding actions”
→
Convert governance discussion into closed, dated, owned actions Open actions with no owner are the most common repeat finding in any audit.

Automating the four repetitive governance tasks

Every recurring task in training governance is a memory task until you make it a system task. Here are the four I removed from my own head.

  • Refresher expiry — calculated from completion date and refresher period, with a 90-day early warning

Fewer chase emails · no surprise expiries · an audit pack that's current on any given Tuesday

The annual training materials & assessment review

Nobody asked me to do this. I introduced a structured annual review cycle in which every mandatory module and its exam is re-validated by its subject-matter owner before the year's programme goes live.

  • A controlled register — every module logged with its material, exam, last-reviewed date and named owner, so staleness is visible at a glance
  • Owner-routed review — each pillar SME receives the specific rows they own and a hard deadline ahead of launch
  • Gaps flagged, not hidden — modules with no material or no assessment are marked explicitly instead of quietly running on
  • Deadline tied to launch — materials are updated before the training window opens, never retrofitted afterwards
  • Change log — the record of what was actually revised, by whom, and when
Every mandatory module carries a named owner and a last-reviewed date before the annual programme launches

Content Governance Workflow

A six-stage procedure covering how learning content enters, stays current in, and exits the catalog.

  • Named ownership — no course enters the catalog without one accountable individual; never a team name, never a job title alone
  • Separated sign-off — the administrator never signs off technical accuracy, the SME never bypasses the governance check
  • Controlled retirement — content is inactivated, not deleted, with the reason and superseding course recorded
  • A content register — owner, last-reviewed date, expiry configuration, assessment and status for every course
  • Health measures — reported to the governance forum alongside completion rate

Upload SOP

A step-by-step operating procedure taking an administrator from a file on the desktop to a published, enrolled course.

  • Media storage first — content is uploaded to the governed drive before it can be attached to any lesson, for business continuity
  • Course type as a governance decision — version-controlled course types for regulatory content, on demand for stable content
  • Expiry configuration — set so the system generates retraining assignments automatically instead of manual chasing
  • A 14-point pre-publish checklist — the compensating control where an administrator builds directly and no system approval is triggered
  • Troubleshooting table — the five most common failure symptoms, mapped to their actual causes
Written so a new administrator can run the system correctly on day one — and so an auditor can see the control, not just the outcome.

The regulated-industry advantage

Most people survive an annual audit. I work to ten a year.

Food manufacturing is governed simultaneously by food safety law, occupational safety law, environmental law, customer standards and corporate policy — and training is in scope in every single audit. Four questions come up every time.

What the auditor examinesHow I evidence it
Does a defined training programme exist, and is it controlled? A signed, version-controlled Training and Development Program with annual review history, controlled distribution, and every change routed through a Document Change Notice.
Is the training content itself current? An annual materials and assessment review I initiated and run: each module and exam re-validated by its named subject-matter owner, with the last-updated date held in a controlled register and the deadline set ahead of programme launch.
Was training actually delivered — and was it reachable by the people who needed it? Digital delivery accessible from factory tablets and registered mobile phones, plus face-to-face registers with trainer signatures. Roughly 80% online, 20% face-to-face.
Can you prove competence, and retrieve the record on demand? Examination results against a defined passing score, effectiveness evaluated through Learn–Use–Teach–Inspect, and records retained for three years in a structured SharePoint repository.

About

Hello — I'm Zarah.

I've spent 15+ years in food manufacturing, most recently as a People Development Specialist, where I own the site Training & Development Programme end to end: the controlled document, the annual calendar, the content review cycle, the LMS administration, the records, and the evidence pack that gets handed to auditors.

What shaped me most was the audit frequency. When training records are examined by a different body every few weeks, you stop treating compliance as a project with a deadline and start treating it as a system that has to hold on any random day.

“Most compliance failures are access failures wearing a different costume.”

That line came from the hardest problem I've solved — getting mandatory training to 280 people who had no company account and no assigned computer. Once they could reach the training and see their own score from the factory floor, compliance stopped being something I chased and started being something that simply happened.

The other habit I'd bring is a suspicion of content that nobody has looked at in a while. Perfect attendance against an outdated deck is still a gap, which is why I put the annual materials review in place rather than waiting for an auditor to ask — and why I write the procedure down rather than keeping it in my head.

I'm precise with records, persistent with follow-up, and genuinely happy when a system I've built means nobody has to ask me for a report again.
